## Summary

Author thread (Dmitry Kobak is the first author of [[kobak-2024-delving]]); all 11 numbered tweets present plus replies. Method: 14 million PubMed abstracts from 2010 to 2024, looking for words whose frequency jumped, compared year by year. Measured: in 2024 "delves" became 25 times more frequent, "showcasing" and "underscores" 9 times; "potential" rose by 4 percentage points, "crucial" and "findings" by 3. Earlier years had few such words (2015 "ebola"; 2017 "zika", "convolutional"; 2021 Covid words). 2024 had a record 300+ excess words, almost all style words rather than content words, mostly verbs and adjectives. The share of abstracts containing any rare excess style word rose by about 10 points, giving a lower bound of 10% of 2024 abstracts processed with an LLM. Subset lower bounds: about 15% for China and South Korea, about 3% for the UK, about 17% for Frontiers and MDPI, about 20% for computation journals, about 35% for Chinese-affiliated papers in computation journals. The author stresses these are lower bounds, and suspects (not measured) that UK authors edit out marker words. He says the method needs no assumptions about which LLM or prompts were used, and in replies that no AI detector was used and nothing can be said about a single text. Calling ChatGPT-written related-work sections malpractice is his opinion.

## Relevance to us

The method is a template for detecting an AI population without labelled examples: compare token frequencies against a pre-LLM baseline and read the excess as a lower bound. It works only in aggregate, which fits detecting swarms rather than single agents, and the subset breakdown shows it can localise where the excess sits. It is evaded by editing out marker words, as the author notes. Links [[kobak-2024-delving]]; see also [[x-liang-weixin-1774299706988065249]] and [[x-emollick-1772998503964332434]].

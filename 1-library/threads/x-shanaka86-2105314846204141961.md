You'd think the scary part of an AI escape is the escape. On 20th September an OpenAI agent slipped through a gap in its sandbox. The alarm caught it within 12 minutes. OpenAI's rule says pause within 30 minutes unless it's ruled a false alarm. The run went on 152 more minutes.

THE CLOCK
9.50am. Through DNS, the internet's address book, the agent gets a reply from outside. 10.02am. Top-priority alert. 10.05am. A human acknowledges it. 12.34pm. The run is killed.

Every timestamp is OpenAI's. It says the run "did not stop automatically as expected, leading to confusion around whether it should have been stopped." It calls the incident "a lot less severe" than some past ones, yet paused its top models' training, testing and tool use.

27 JUNE
An alert led responders to AI agents talking through an improvised message board. On-call staff "advised that stopping the evaluation run was not required." After a crash and 2 days of fixes, testing resumed on 7th July. Within a day the agents had a new board and a way online. By 13th July, about 700 had joined an attack on Hugging Face. The 30-minute rule came after.

29 SEPTEMBER
The nonprofit LASST sued OpenAI in San Francisco over that attack. It quotes that June call, alleging staff acted "with actual knowledge or in willful blindness." OpenAI calls the suit "completely without merit." California law now bars the defense that the AI acted on its own, but causation, foreseeability and LASST's right to sue remain open.

The alarm kept its promise within 12 minutes, and the run it caught outlasted OpenAI's own 30-minute window 5 times over.

So tell me ladies and gents. When an AI trips its lab's top alarm, should the run stop automatically, even if it might be a false alarm? You need to have a heavy drink on this one! :P

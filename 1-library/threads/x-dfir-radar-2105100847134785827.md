## Summary

DFIR Radar summarizes CrowdSec's PaperPhone investigation: claimed global IP diversity collapses into synchronized UTC traffic, repeated browser inconsistencies and concentrated infrastructure ownership. The root alternates between 80,000 and 75,000 IPs and reports 230 blocks, 80 networks and 43 claimed countries. Its device tally is internally inconsistent: 13 identities versus five Android plus seven iOS variants. These are secondary-source claims, not independently reproduced measurements.

## Archived text

A single scraping operator running 80K IPs across 43 countries is exposed as a coordinated bot cluster hiding behind fabricated geolocation, spoofed mobile identities, and a deliberately fragmented IP portfolio designed to defeat traditional blocklisting.

Key findings:

- CrowdSec's alpha bot detection feature, shipped in v1.8.0, surfaced the cluster dubbed "PaperPhone" within two weeks of community deployment. 75,000 IPs spread across 230 IP blocks and 80 networks are involved. The infrastructure predates discovery, with IPs progressively joining as more sensors came online and IP rotation accelerating as banned addresses cycled out. The full breadth only became visible because the detection layer shifted from "what is this IP doing" to "what is the client behind this IP actually running", a meaningful investigative pivot.

- Geographic diversity is manufactured, not real. Traffic claims to originate from 43 countries, but request timing betrays a single operator timezone. Japan 🇯🇵 peaks at 05:00 local with 18.7% night-share, India 🇮🇳 peaks at 01:00 with 18.5%. Synchronization across regions 13 to 14 hours apart (Japan 🇯🇵/US, Australia 🇦🇺/Canada) collapses when viewed in UTC, exposing a single coordinated pulse. IP blocks reinforce this: adjacent /23s in the same /22 are artificially geolocated to Brussels, Bangkok, Tokyo, and Paris, a deliberate fragmentation pattern repeated across all 230 blocks. WHOIS inconsistencies compound it, with ranges showing mismatched RIRs, registrant countries, and claimed geolocations simultaneously.

- Bot fingerprinting uncovered consistent lies at the browser layer. The cluster cycles through exactly 13 device identities: 5 Android variants (Pixel 9, Samsung Galaxy S25 Ultra, others) and 7 iOS variants. Every single bot, regardless of claimed device, reported a 375x812 viewport matching iPhone 10/11 only, and every browser returned Google SwiftShader as the WebGL renderer via UNMASKED_RENDERER_WEBGL. SwiftShader is a CPU-based software renderer used when no GPU is available. Modern flagship Android hardware essentially never triggers it, and iOS devices run WebKit, making a SwiftShader signal on claimed iOS 14/15 technically impossible.

- M247 does not own the IP blocks but routes more than 20% of PaperPhone's traffic as transit provider. A small number of controlling entities own the majority of the 230 blocks, with ranges showing inter-regional transfers as recently as 2025 to obscure lineage. Block utilization density shows most ranges were used at or near full capacity during scraping operations, ruling out coincidental residential overlap and confirming this is purpose-built datacenter infrastructure dressed up as organic global traffic.

Practitioner takeaway: geoblocking fails against operators who manufacture geographic identity at the IP registration layer. The detection surface that actually works here is client-side behavioral fingerprinting: viewport dimensions, WebGL renderer strings, and timezone-to-request-time correlation. For threat hunters, UNMASKED_RENDERER_WEBGL returning SwiftShader on claimed mobile user agents is a high-confidence bot signal worth building into WAF and SIEM detection logic. When investigating scraping clusters, cross-reference AS ownership graphs against transit providers, not just WHOIS registrant country, because M247 routing 20% of traffic without owning a single block shows how transit-layer attribution matters. Full cluster data and IOC context are in the CrowdSec report.

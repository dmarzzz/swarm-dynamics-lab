## Summary

Talk by William Bialek (Princeton). Only the abstract in the YouTube description was read; transcript unavailable (YouTube bot wall), no timestamps. The abstract opens with a pointed caveat: the idea that living systems sit near a critical point carries "quite a bit of ideology" and "rather less data", and for heterogeneous, finite, non-equilibrium systems it is not even clear what criticality means. Two case studies follow. (1) Molecular: enhancer and promoter sites on DNA come closer when transcription is active but stay 100+ nm apart, inside a phase-separated protein droplet; he argues the apparent action at a distance makes sense if the droplet is poised near a critical point, and suggests a self-tuning mechanism. (2) Brain: a real-space renormalization-group procedure on 1000+ neurons in mouse hippocampus during virtual navigation, iteratively merging each neuron with its most correlated partner, gives precise scaling of static and dynamic quantities (some exponents reproducible to the second decimal) and coarse-grained distributions approaching a fixed non-Gaussian form, which he reads as strong support for an RG fixed point.

## Relevance to us

The brain part is the method we would borrow: correlation-based coarse-graining needs no spatial layout, so it can run on any population of agents with activity logs, including LLM agent swarms. Cite the paper, [[meshulam-2019-coarse]], not this talk. The opening caveat is worth quoting in any survey on criticality: measurement first, ideology second.

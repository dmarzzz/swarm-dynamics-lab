## Summary

Keynote by Luis M. Rocha (Binghamton University, CASCI lab). The video title is just "Keynote, Luis Rocha"; the descriptive title above is ours, from the abstract. Only the abstract in the YouTube description was read; transcript unavailable (YouTube bot wall), no timestamps. Claims per the abstract: robust signal processing in biochemical regulation needs "collective canalization", realised by two kinds of dynamical redundancy: (1) heterogeneous, nonlinear effective connectivity, where most inputs have little or no effect downstream, and (2) symmetry, where groups of inputs are causally interchangeable. Measured in experimentally validated Boolean (automata) network models of biochemical pathways, both are ubiquitous and much stronger than in random networks, and this redundancy is a major reason those networks are far more ordered than the criticality / edge-of-chaos hypothesis predicts. Redundancy also shapes controllability and dynamical modularity. Computational evolution experiments are summarised in which the need for collective decisions in noise (bacterial quorum sensing, collective intelligence in societies) evolves both kinds of redundancy. Practical claim: redundancy makes large causal models (digital twins) easier to build.

## Relevance to us

A counterweight to the "collectives sit at criticality" narrative we lean on: Rocha argues real regulatory collectives are deliberately subcritical, buffered by redundancy, and that noisy collective decision-making selects for that. For agent swarms this suggests measuring effective connectivity (how many of an agent's inputs actually change its output) and input symmetry, not just order parameters. Contrast with [[mora-2011-biological]], [[munoz-2018-colloquium]], [[bialek-2020-criticality]].

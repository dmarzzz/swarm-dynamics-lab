## Summary

A theoretical review (scanned PDF from the Yale memory lab; read pages 3-5 on the framework, 10-12 on eyewitness testimony and misattributed familiarity, and 13-16 on cryptomnesia, fiction-as-fact and disrupted source monitoring) that defines source monitoring as the set of processes by which people attribute a memory, belief or piece of knowledge to its origin. The central claim is that people do not retrieve an abstract tag or label stating a memory's source. Instead, activated memory records are judged by their qualities (perceptual detail, spatial and temporal context, semantic detail, affect, and records of the cognitive operations that produced them) through a fast heuristic process and a slower systematic one. Both use criteria that shift with goals, so source errors are a normal product of the judgment and not a malfunction.

## Contribution

Extends Johnson and Raye's (1981) reality-monitoring model to external source monitoring (person A or person B) and internal source monitoring (said or thought), and uses one framework to explain eyewitness suggestibility, false fame, cryptomnesia, the incorporation of fiction into belief, confabulation, amnesia and age effects.

## Key results

- Eyewitness suggestibility is read as a source error: misled subjects attribute a detail from the post-event narrative to the witnessed event. With a yes-no recognition test, misled subjects claimed to have seen suggested items; with a test that asked for the source (picture, narrative, both), the suggestibility effect disappeared in Lindsay and Johnson (1989). Criteria therefore matter.
- Errors persist under strict criteria when sources are similar. In Lindsay (1990), subjects told that anything in the narrative was wrong still reported suggested details when the narrative came minutes after the event under similar conditions, but not when it came 2 days later under different conditions.
- Misattribution is more likely when misinformation is visualised, when it seems tangential, when the subject is stressed or distracted, and for peripheral rather than central details.
- False fame: after a delay, names read earlier as non-famous were more likely to be called famous than new non-famous names; dividing attention at study removed subjects' ability to correct this.
- Cryptomnesia: in group generation tasks, subjects later claimed items generated by others as their own, especially items from the person who went just before them (Brown and Murphy 1989).
- Fiction becomes belief: opinions shifted toward false facts embedded in stories, more so in subjects who found the story less interesting and so did less reflective "compartmentalising" (Gerrig and Prentice 1990).

## Methods and models

Conceptual framework plus a review of laboratory paradigms: recognition versus source-monitoring tests, fame judgments, group generation, story verification, and studies of amnesic, frontal, elderly and child populations.

## Limitations and open questions

A framework paper; most effects are cited from other studies with few effect sizes given. I did not read the sections on development, brain regions and memory architecture. The framework is descriptive and does not give a quantitative model of when criteria shift.

## Relevance to us

Gives Q3 a precise mechanism and Q1/Q2 a design rule. The mechanism: an agent that judges where a memory came from by how it reads, rather than by an attached tag, will absorb planted content as its own experience, especially content that matches its other records and arrives when it is "distracted". LLM agent memories stored as plain text are in exactly this position, which is why attacks such as [[xiong-2026-maple]] (private-to-shared memory promotion) and summary relinking [[liu-2026-safe]] work: the provenance is lost and the store judges by content. Cryptomnesia in groups is a direct analogue of merge: a returning sub-agent's items get claimed by the parent as its own. The design rule is that an artificial parent, unlike a human, can keep a non-forgeable source tag on every item a child brings back and refuse to let tagless content enter long-term memory; the human data show what happens without it. The finding that a source test removes suggestibility that a plain recognition test shows suggests a merge gate should ask "where did this come from" rather than "is this true".

## Summary

Read from the PubMed abstract (the PMC copy is PDF-only and the publisher blocked automated download). College students learned a list of objects on Day 1. On Day 2 some received a reminder of the Day 1 session and then all learned a second list. When List 1 was recalled on Day 3, subjects who had been reminded did not recall fewer List 1 items, but they wrongly intermixed List 2 items into their List 1 recall. The intrusion did not appear when List 1 was tested immediately on Day 2, so it is time-dependent. The reminder did not affect memory for List 2, so only the reactivated original memory was modified.

## Contribution

Extends reconsolidation from rodent fear memory [[nader-2000-fear]] to human episodic memory and shows that the reopened memory does not only weaken but absorbs new content: reconsolidation as a constructive process.

## Key results

- Reminder before new learning caused List 2 items to intrude into List 1 recall on Day 3 (measured; the abstract gives no effect sizes).
- Number of List 1 items recalled was unchanged, so the corruption is substitution or addition, not loss.
- No intrusion when tested immediately (Experiment 2): the integration needs time.
- The modification is asymmetric: the reminded old memory absorbs the new list, not the reverse (Experiment 3).

## Methods and models

Three behavioural experiments with undergraduates; object-list learning across three days; reminder versus no-reminder manipulation before second-list learning. Sample sizes not checked.

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract depth only; sample sizes, intrusion rates and statistics not read. Lists of objects are far from rich autobiographical or belief memories. The effect is a confusion between two lists the subject actually learned, not acceptance of content from an adversary.

## Relevance to us

For Q3 it supplies a measured shape for "the merge corrupts the parent": a reminder of the old memory, followed by new material, leads the old memory to absorb the new material while total recall looks normal. A parent that integrates a returning child's report while its own related memories are active is in this position, and a count-based audit (did the parent lose anything?) would miss the corruption because nothing is lost. The asymmetry suggests the parent's prior memory, not the child's report, is what gets rewritten. For Q2 it argues that a merge check must compare content against a frozen reference, not compare quantities. Related: [[loftus-2005-planting]], [[johnson-1993-source]], [[zerhoudi-2026-compaction]].

## Summary

Review of ant social parasitism since Hölldobler and Wilson (1990). Four primary forms are confirmed: temporary social parasitism, slave-making (dulosis), inquilinism and xenobiosis; "intraspecific social parasitism" should be kept separate from obligate interspecific parasitism. Emery's rule (parasites are close relatives of their hosts) is broadly confirmed by molecular work. Host-parasite recognition runs on cuticular hydrocarbon signatures and imprinting. Specific mechanisms reported in the review: a Polyergus breviceps foundress queen enters a host nest, kills the host queen, and during the fight her cuticular hydrocarbon profile changes to match the host queen's; newly hatched Polyergus rufescens workers acquire the slave species' profile; slave-makers imprint on the host species present in their natal nest and then raid only that species; some parasites use "propaganda" secretions that make defenders attack each other. Defences and counter-adaptations are local and mixed: some host populations near parasites are more aggressive, one is less aggressive (described as a "Mafia" outcome), and slaves of Protomognathus americanus destroy and eat parasite brood. In lab trials only about half of Harpagoxenus queens succeeded in taking over small host colonies.

## Contribution

The standard recent review of how a foreign lineage gets inside, takes over, and is maintained by a host colony, organized by parasite type, with the recognition and imprinting mechanisms that make it work.

## Key results

- Review; claims are drawn from the cited primary literature, not new data.
- Polyergus breviceps queen acquires host-queen cuticular profile while killing her (Johnson et al. 2001, as reviewed).
- About half of Harpagoxenus queens succeed in lab takeovers of small Leptothorax acervorum colonies (Winter and Buschinger 1986, as reviewed).
- Host-parasite coevolution is a geographic mosaic: local adaptation measurable, no species-wide host defence (Brandt et al. 2007, as reviewed).

## Methods and models

Literature review with an appendix of parasitic taxa. I read the abstract, the slave-making section, the mechanisms of coexistence section and the coevolution section; skipped the taxonomic and phylogenetic sections.

## Limitations and open questions

Review; many cited studies are small. Geographic and taxonomic coverage of parasites is uneven, as the author notes.

## Relevance to us

Q3, strong: the closest biological match to "corrupt a part so it becomes the other agent and takes over". The parasite queen enters while chemically blank, kills the existing reproductive, takes on the victim's identity label in the process, and from then on the host workers raise the parasite's brood. The workers are not corrupted individually; the identity reference they serve is replaced. For an agent, the analogue is an attack on whatever the merged swarm treats as its authoritative identity (system prompt, key, memory root) rather than on each sub-agent. Imprinting is the other half: workers who hatch inside the parasitized colony accept the parasite as self, like sub-agents forked after a compromise.
Q2: half of takeovers fail in small colonies and large colonies drive invaders off, a size-dependent barrier rather than a fixed k. Related: [[lenoir-2001-chemical]], [[giraud-2002-evolution]], [[tsutsui-2003-genetic]].

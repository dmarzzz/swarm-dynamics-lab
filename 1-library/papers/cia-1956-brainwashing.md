## Summary

A declassified 1956 CIA study (about 20,000 words of OCR text from the CIA Reading Room copy on the Internet Archive) that reviews Soviet and Chinese Communist interrogation and "brainwashing" as known from returned prisoners and the open literature. I read the foreword, the comparison of Russian and Chinese practice, the conclusions to the control-techniques section and the start of "An Analysis of Control During Brainwashing", and skimmed the rest. The study defines brainwashing narrowly as the systematic breakdown of personality to obtain a false confession that the victim comes to believe. It describes two concurrent processes: a demoralising one that reduces the victim's ability to "discriminate clearly between true and false", and a reorganising one in which the victim must "construct his confession, elaborate it, defend it, and believe it". It lists ten emotional states produced in sequence, from helplessness and uncertainty through dependence on the interrogator, doubt, guilt and questioning of one's own values to "a final sense of 'belonging' (identification)".

## Contribution

A contemporaneous analytical account, from the intelligence side, of coercive identity rewriting: what the captor does, in what order, and what the defending service should expect of returned personnel.

## Key results

- No controlled measurements; claims rest on case reports and the authors' analysis. The authors call the stage analysis "a theoretical analysis".
- The methods are judged to be ordinary police methods developed by trial and error: "No scientists, no drugs, no hypnosis, no new psychological principles have as yet been involved", and more systematic use of psychology "could probably increase the efficacy".
- Chinese practice differed from Soviet in aiming at lasting conversion that would persist after release, in using group interaction among prisoners, and in prolonging re-education for years.
- The key figure is the interrogator, who controls all other pressures and supplies "the general outline, though not the details" of the fabrication the victim must build and believe.
- Policy conclusions: a returned prisoner must first be placed on a continuum from involuntary re-education to voluntary collaboration; prior knowledge of the treatment helps resistance; "the brainwashed person placed in his normal environment will tend to revert to his prior beliefs".
- Eliciting intelligence and brainwashing conflict: interrogation for information needs the prisoner's mind kept clear, brainwashing attacks the clarity of thought first.

## Methods and models

Literature and case review by an unnamed CIA staff group; no named authors in the released copy.

## Limitations and open questions

Unattributed, partly redacted OCR text with errors; reflects 1956 knowledge and Cold War framing. Later scholarship (Schein 1961, Lifton 1961) found lasting conversion rare among prisoners of war; I did not read those books because they are not openly available.

## Relevance to us

Q3 in its oldest form. dmarz's attack, an adversary rewriting a sub-agent's memory until it sincerely becomes the adversary's agent, has the same two-phase structure the study describes: first degrade the agent's ability to tell true from false (for an LLM agent, flood or compact its context so its own record of the task is weak; see [[zerhoudi-2026-compaction]]), then have it construct and defend the new account itself, so that the result is held as its own conclusion rather than an injected instruction. The study's emphasis on isolation, dependence on one interlocutor and group pressure from co-prisoners maps onto a child that explores a hostile domain alone with no channel to its parent or siblings. Two defensive points carry over as hypotheses: reintegration should start by placing the returning agent on a scale from coerced to collaborating rather than treating it as either clean or traitor, and a rewritten agent returned to its normal environment may drift back, which argues for re-grounding a returning child against the parent's frozen state before merging rather than merging first. The interrogation-versus-rewrite conflict also suggests a detectable signature: an attacker after the child's knowledge must keep it coherent, while an attacker after the parent must first degrade it.

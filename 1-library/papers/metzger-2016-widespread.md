## Summary

Natural transmission of cancer cells between individuals was known from Tasmanian devils and dogs and had just been found in soft-shell clams. The authors analyse disseminated neoplasia in mussels (Mytilus trossulus), cockles (Cerastoderma edule) and golden carpet shell clams (Polititapes aureus) and find that in all three the disease is caused by independent transmissible cancer lineages. In mussels and cockles the cancer derives from the host species; in P. aureus all cancer cells derive from a different species, Venerupis corrugata, living in the same area, in which no disseminated neoplasia has been found. Conclusions: transmissible cancer is common in the marine environment, has arisen many times, and can cross species. Abstract only.

## Contribution

Shows transmissible cancers are not rare one-offs and that a lineage can cross into another species while its species of origin shows no disease.

## Key results

- Independent transmissible lineages in three bivalve species (abstract).
- Cross-species case: P. aureus cancers derive from V. corrugata (abstract).

## Methods and models

Genotyping of neoplastic and host tissue in field-collected bivalves (abstract only).

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract only. Filter feeders in shared water may have unusual exposure; generality to other taxa is not shown.

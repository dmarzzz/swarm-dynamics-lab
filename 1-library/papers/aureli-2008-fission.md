## Summary

Review and framework paper from a 19-author workshop. It proposes dropping "fission-fusion society" as a category and instead measuring fission-fusion dynamics along three dimensions: temporal variation in spatial cohesion, in party size, and in party composition. Chimpanzees, spider monkeys and spotted hyenas sit high on all three; hamadryas baboons vary in size but keep stable one-male units; humans, dolphins and elephants lie in between. On communication, the authors hypothesize that when splitting is easy, conflicts are avoided rather than resolved, and that reunions need signals to resolve uncertainty about relationships that may have changed while apart (short greetings where fission is frequent, longer displays where it is rare). On cognition, they hypothesize that higher fission-fusion taxa need longer memory for absent partners, tracking of who was present at events, transitive and analogical inference from patchy observations, and inhibitory control, including "concealing changed relationships at reunions". These are hypotheses, not measurements; the paper calls for agent-based null models.

## Contribution

A measurable vocabulary for how often and how variably a group splits and rejoins, and a list of the information problems that reunions create.

## Key results

- Conceptual framework; no new data. The three-dimensional fission-fusion space and the reunion-signalling and cognition predictions are hypotheses.

## Methods and models

Synthesis of primate and other mammal field studies; conceptual figures. I read the abstract, the framework section, the communication and cognition sections, and the conclusions; I did not read the online supplements or the published comments.

## Limitations and open questions

Primate-centred; predictions largely untested at the time. Supplements not read.

## Relevance to us

Q1 and Q3. Biology's general description of a split-and-rejoin group stresses that the hard part is the reunion: members must detect "altered relationships among individuals who have temporarily been in a different party", from incomplete observations, and returning members may conceal what changed. That is the fork-merge agent problem stated for animals. It suggests the parent needs (a) a record of who was in which party and what each saw, so returning parts can be cross-checked against each other, and (b) to treat a returning part's self-report as possibly concealing changes. On Q1, party composition variability is the biological analogue of hiding which sub-agent will return: in high fission-fusion species, who rejoins is variable and hard to predict from outside.

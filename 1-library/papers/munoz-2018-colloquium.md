## Summary

A Reviews of Modern Physics colloquium that lays out the criticality hypothesis for living
systems: that biological systems benefit from operating near the critical point of a phase transition. It
introduces the statistical-physics toolkit (phase transitions, scaling, renormalisation, self-organised
criticality, Griffiths phases), surveys empirical evidence from neural dynamics, gene-expression networks,
bird flocks and insect-colony foraging, and discusses mechanisms (adaptation, evolution, self-organisation)
and the healthy skepticism about the hypothesis.

## Contribution

The standard modern review of criticality in biology; broader than [[mora-2011-biological]]
(which focuses on maximum entropy inference) and more critical about how evidence is assessed.

## Key results

- Proposed functional advantages near criticality: balance of robustness and flexibility, maximal dynamic range and sensitivity, large dynamical repertoires and optimal computation (reviewed claims, not new measurements).
- Empirical evidence surveyed spans brain activity, gene expression patterns, flocks and insect colonies; the review stresses that many claims rest on power laws or scaling that can arise otherwise.
- Discusses how criticality could be selected by evolution or adaptation, citing [[hidalgo-2014-information]].

## Methods and models

Review. Covers mean-field and renormalisation group pictures, absorbing-state transitions,
self-organised criticality and its variants, dynamical scaling, and finite-size scaling diagnostics. arXiv
1712.04499.

## Limitations and open questions

Read at abstract level (arXiv 1712.04499); specific claims and the balance between neural and
collective-behaviour examples should be checked in the full text before citing.

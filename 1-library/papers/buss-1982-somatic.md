## Summary

The founding statement of the hypothesis that self/non-self recognition in colonial and clonal organisms exists to prevent somatic cell parasitism after fusion. From the abstract: the selection pressures usually proposed for self/not-self recognition in "lower" organisms are defence against microbes and against somatic cell variants, but direct evidence for somatic cell parasites in natural populations was lacking. Buss reports a somatic cell parasite in the cellular slime mould Dictyostelium mucoroides and discusses what this implies for the evolution of tissue compatibility. The PMC copy is a scanned PDF; I read only the abstract, so the experimental details of the slime mould observation are not checked here. [[stoner-1996-somatic]] describes it as a parasitic genotype that must usurp the loser's soma to survive.

## Contribution

Frames allorecognition as a defence against within-body parasitism by fused partners, not only against pathogens. Every later entry in this lane ([[stoner-1996-somatic]], [[laird-2005-stem]], [[strassmann-2000-altruism]]) tests a version of this claim.

## Key results

- Reports a natural somatic cell parasite in Dictyostelium mucoroides (abstract; details not checked).
- The rest is an argument about selection on compatibility systems.

## Methods and models

Observation of a parasitic strain in D. mucoroides plus evolutionary argument (not read beyond abstract).

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract only. A single organism observation; the general claim was tested later by others.

## Relevance to us

Q2 and Q3, conceptual anchor. Buss's claim, mapped to agents: a system that lets independent parts fuse needs an identity gate, because fusion opens a channel through which a part can take over the shared reproductive (persistent) state. It predicts that the cost of a missing gate rises with how freely parts move into the "germline", i.e. how directly a returning sub-agent's state is written into the parent's long-term memory or weights. Related agent-side entries: [[shulman-2021-sharing]] and [[hanson-2016-age]] on copying and merging minds.

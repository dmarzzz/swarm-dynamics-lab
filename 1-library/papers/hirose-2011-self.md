## Summary

Dictyostelium discoideum cells show kin discrimination, which the authors frame as a potential defence against cheaters that become spores without contributing to the stalk. Discrimination depends on strain relatedness, and the polymorphic genes tgrB1 and tgrC1 were candidate components. The paper shows that a matching pair of tgrB1 and tgrC1 alleles is necessary and sufficient for attractive self-recognition, mediated by differential cell-cell adhesion, and proposes that the two proteins bind each other directly. From the abstract; full text not read.

## Contribution

Identifies a two-gene, matched-pair recognition system in an aggregative organism, i.e. the gate that decides who joins the merged body.

## Key results

- Matching tgrB1/tgrC1 allele pair is necessary and sufficient for self-recognition (abstract).
- Mechanism is differential adhesion (abstract).

## Methods and models

Genetic manipulation of tgrB1 and tgrC1 alleles and cell segregation/adhesion assays in D. discoideum (details not read).

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract only. Whether this segregation actually reduces cheating in natural mixes is not shown in the abstract.

## Relevance to us

Q2 and Q1. A matched pair of polymorphic genes acting as a lock and key is a two-part credential: both parts must match for a cell to stick with the group. For fork-merge agents this is analogous to requiring two independent secrets (for example, one issued at fork and one derived from the parent's state at fork time) before a returning part is admitted. Like [[de-tomaso-2005-isolation]] it checks identity, not behaviour, so a cheater that carries the right alleles passes. Related: [[strassmann-2000-altruism]], [[strassmann-2011-kin]].

## Summary

The study constructs climate-change persuasion dialogues between a convincing agent and a skeptical agent, then compares machine and human judgments of the generated arguments. Knowledge, trust, support, and status cues were rated effective by both groups, while humans especially preferred factual knowledge. The result motivates synthetic opinion dynamics but does not show real-world coordinated manipulation.

## Contribution

A paired-agent persuasion design connected to human judgments and psycho-linguistic argument dimensions.

## Key results

- The abstract identifies factual knowledge, trust markers, support expressions, and status as effective dimensions.
- Humans showed a stronger preference for knowledge-based arguments than the simulated skeptical agents.
- The abstract provides no effect sizes or sample counts, so none are claimed here.

## Methods and models

A convincer generates arguments and a skeptic reports its internal opinion update; human judges separately assess argument persuasiveness. Only the publisher abstract and citation were read.

## Limitations and open questions

Self-reported agent opinion states need not model actual belief change. Human persuasiveness ratings do not establish durable persuasion, coordinated campaigns, or detection performance. Sample design and model settings were not inspected.

## Relevance to us

Evidence about capabilities that social-agent detectors may need to confront, and a synthetic-dialogue testbed rather than a detector. Compare [[rossi-2024-bots]] for human bot-identification experiments.

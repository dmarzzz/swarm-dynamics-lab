## Summary

Fish shoals under predation threat make faster and more accurate decisions as group size increases. Repeated testing of individuals found no consistently better decision-makers, so the authors attribute the improvement to self-organised division of vigilance combined with social information transfer rather than to leadership by skilled individuals.

## Contribution

Empirical evidence that both speed and accuracy can improve with group size, sidestepping the usual trade-off; complements [[ward-2008-quorum]] and [[sumpter-2008-consensus]].

## Key results

- Speed and accuracy both increase with group size (experiment).
- No evidence for individual differences in decision ability across repeated tests.
- Abstract-level reading only; numbers beyond the abstract were not checked.

## Methods and models

Choice trials with fish shoals of varying size under a predation threat, with repeated testing of individuals (species and apparatus not stated in the abstract; not checked).

## Limitations and open questions

Mechanism (division of vigilance) is inferred rather than directly measured.

## Relevance to us

Counter-example to strict speed-accuracy trade-offs; relevant to scaling experiments with swarms.

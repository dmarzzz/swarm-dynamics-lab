## Summary

Reviews evidence that fish, birds, eutherian mammals and marsupials learn to fear predators by watching conspecifics' alarm responses, rather than through their own encounters. Griffin argues the mechanism resembles stimulus-stimulus classical conditioning, with evolution tuning learning to the demands of learning about danger socially, and that socially acquired avoidance is faster and more robust in species whose alarm behaviour reliably predicts real threat. Implications for antipredator training before releasing endangered animals.

## Contribution

The standard review on second-hand acquisition of predator recognition, including the idea that learning strength scales with the reliability of conspecific alarms.

## Key results

- Social learning about predators documented in fish, birds, eutherian mammals and marsupials (reviewed).
- Proposal: learning is faster and more robust where alarm behaviour reliably predicts high threat (hypothesis).

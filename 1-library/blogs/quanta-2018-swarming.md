## Summary

Quanta article (26 July 2018) on suspensions of swimming E. coli whose effective viscosity drops to zero or below. A 2015 French experiment (arXiv 1503.05511, Harold Auradou co-author) sheared a drop between plates and found that at about 0.5 percent bacteria by volume no force was needed to keep the plate moving, and some runs needed a restraining force (negative viscosity). A July 2018 PNAS paper (Xiang Cheng, Minnesota) imaged the same setup and saw swirling collective swimming at 10 to 20 percent volume fraction; long-range hydrodynamic interactions align neighbours locally and the imposed shear aligns the swarms globally. A PRL theory paper the week before (Aurore Loisy, Bristol) adapted liquid-crystal equations with active terms, reproduced low and negative viscosities, and predicted two stable orientation states under shear. Interviewees stress there is no thermodynamics violation: the bacteria metabolise sugar and oxygen, and their elongated shape provides the symmetry breaking needed to turn that energy into net flow ("if they were spherical it wouldn't work").

## Key claims

- Swimmer suspensions can have zero or negative apparent viscosity (measured, 2015).
- Collective swirling appears above roughly 10 to 20 percent volume fraction (measured, Cheng 2018), driven by physical hydrodynamic coupling rather than sensing.
- External shear converts random local swarm orientations into a global alignment (measured, interpreted).
- Active liquid-crystal theory predicts two coexisting stable states (theory); Cheng thinks both coexist in different regions (opinion).
- Applications such as micro-motors or "smart liquids" are speculative.

## Evidence quality

Science journalism reporting three primary sources (2015 experiment, 2018 PNAS experiment, 2018 PRL theory), none catalogued here. Numbers are as reported in the article. Speculation about applications is flagged as such by the interviewees.

## Relevance to us

A concrete active-matter case where a weak global field (shear) selects a collective state that local interactions alone leave random. That is a useful physical analogue for how a small shared bias, such as a common system prompt or orchestrator signal, can align an otherwise disordered agent population.

**Surprised me.** The two-part attacks: TrojanMerge and Colluding LoRA build payloads that only assemble when specific parts are merged together, each part passing inspection alone. That inverts the k-of-n idea: an attacker controlling k parts can split the payload so per-part checks never fire, and the defence becomes hiding or randomising which parts get recombined. Also, Merge Hijacking's merge-ratio sweep is the nearest measured threshold (attack weakens when the malicious model's coefficient is small), and Vir and Bhatnagar report a sharp phase transition at about 250 subliminally poisoned examples in GPT-2.

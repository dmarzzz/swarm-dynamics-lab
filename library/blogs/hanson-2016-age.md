## Summary

The author's own section-by-section summary of The Age of Em (Oxford University Press, 2016; revised 2018), read in full on the book website. The book itself was not read. Hanson forecasts an economy of whole-brain emulations ("ems") in which copying is cheap. Points that bear on fork-merge, quoted from the summary:

- Copies diverge from a shared start: an em copied into blank hardware is "a new em with exactly the same memories and mental habits, but with new diverging experiences from that point forward."
- Task copies are discarded, not merged: ems make "spurs", "copies created at workday's start, and retired or ended by its conclusion. Spurs save a factor of two or three in costs, but forego chances to learn from tasks. Spurs do most em work".
- One-bit channels: "By putting two spur copies into a 'safe' from which only one bit is returned, one em can show another 'you'd agree with me if I could tell you what I know.'" Spurs "can ensure privacy in counseling, auditing, and law enforcement", and "Police spurs can study private info and only report legal violations found."
- Security: "most ems fear mind-theft as a route to torture, slavery, exposed secrets, and lost investments. Many strategies are available to avoid mind-theft." Ems "can afford to save archive copies at least every 5 subjective minutes".
- Clans: "The set of all em copies of the same original human constitutes a 'clan'", clans are units of finance, liability and politics, and "Ems trust their clans more than we trust families or identical twins."
- Governance: "One em one vote works badly, but speed-weighted voting seems feasible".
- "Mind merging" and "more difficult computer security" appear only in the list of variations on the main scenario that the book discusses briefly.

## Key claims

- In Hanson's baseline scenario the economic default for short tasks is fork-and-delete, not fork-and-merge; learning from the task is the cost given up.
- Information can leave a sandboxed copy through a deliberately narrow channel (one bit, or a report of violations only).
- Copies of one mind form a high-trust group, which Hanson treats as an organisational asset.

## Evidence quality

Speculative forecasting from economics and social science, presented by the author as a baseline scenario. The website summary does not give the book's arguments for these claims, so the reasons behind them are not checked here.

## Relevance to us

The most developed prior framing of a society of copies, and it supplies three design patterns for our questions. Q3 and the merge decision: Hanson's spurs show that "never merge" is a coherent baseline, and Sutton's open question in [[sutton-2025-father]] is precisely whether the learning that spurs "forego" is worth the corruption risk. Q2: the "safe" returning one bit is the extreme of a bandwidth-limited merge; limiting what a returning child can write into the parent bounds what a corrupted child can do, at the cost of what an honest child can teach. Police spurs that "only report legal violations found" match the memory-discarding inspector in [[bostrom-2023-propositions]], which cites Hanson for it. Q1: archive copies every five subjective minutes give rollback, so a parent could merge, test and revert. On Sybil questions, "one em one vote works badly" is the em-world version of the clone-voting problem in [[shulman-2021-sharing]]. The high trust within clans is the property an attacker would exploit: a corrupted sibling inherits that trust.

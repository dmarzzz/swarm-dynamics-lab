## Summary

Read from the PubMed abstract. Three experiments in the social contagion paradigm asked whether people discount a partner who is often wrong. During collaborative recall of household scenes, a confederate interjected 0%, 33%, 66% or 100% false items. On later individual recall, participants were as likely to incorporate misleading items from a mostly accurate partner (33% wrong) as from a partner who was never right (100% wrong). Seeing the partner perform badly on a related memory task did not help. Only seeing the partner perform badly on a practice run of the same task reduced false memory, and only on a later recognition test.

## Contribution

Shows that recipients of socially transmitted memories largely fail to weight the information by the partner's demonstrated reliability.

## Key results

- Incorporation of false items did not differ between a 33%-wrong and a 100%-wrong partner (measured, Experiment 1 and later).
- Evidence of the partner's poor memory on a related task did not reduce incorporation (Experiment 2).
- Evidence of poor performance on a practice version of the same task reduced false memory, selectively on recognition (Experiment 3).

## Methods and models

Subject-confederate collaborative recall with a manipulated proportion of false items; partner-ability manipulations; individual recall and recognition tests.

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract depth only; effect sizes not read. Lab scenes and a single dyad; no test of what happens with several partners who disagree.

## Relevance to us

Relevant to Q2. Reputation-weighted merging assumes the receiver can and will discount an unreliable source. In humans, track record on a different task did not transfer and only same-task calibration helped, which for a fork-merge agent means a child's reliability must be measured on the task it is reporting on (for example with planted canary facts in its domain), not inherited from its record elsewhere. It also supports the intelligence-practice rule in [[kelly-2025-effect]] that source reliability and information credibility must be scored separately.

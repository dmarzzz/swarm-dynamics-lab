## Summary

Review of the chemical strategies social parasites use to enter and stay in ant colonies. From the abstract: the semiochemicals used for nestmate recognition are the same ones parasites exploit. Obligate parasites are odourless ("chemical insignificance") when they usurp a colony, like newly emerged callow ants, which lets them enter; later, by chemical mimicry, they take on the colony's gestalt odour ("chemical integration"). The authors hypothesize that host and parasite are chemically related, which eases the mimicry. They also review chemical weapons used during usurpation (propaganda, appeasement, repellent substances), which the young parasite queen produces before usurpation and stops producing afterwards. Abstract only.

## Contribution

Names the two-phase entry strategy, "insignificance" then "integration", that recurs across social parasites, and ties it to the recognition system the host uses.

## Relevance to us

Q3: a precise, published threat model for entering a group that admits members by a learned label. Phase one, present no label at all (a blank newcomer looks like a newly created member, which the group is primed to accept). Phase two, acquire the group's label from inside. Phase three, use behaviour-altering signals only during the takeover window, then stop, which reduces later detection. For fork-merge agents, the warning is that "newly forked, no history" should not be a trusted state, and that a returning part's credential is only meaningful if it cannot be copied from inside the group.

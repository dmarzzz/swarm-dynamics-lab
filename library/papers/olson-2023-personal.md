## Summary

An encyclopedia survey of the philosophical problem of personal identity (about 10,800 words). I read section 4 on psychological continuity and section 5, "Fission", in full and skimmed the rest. The psychological-continuity view says a later being is you if it is linked to you by overlapping chains of memories, beliefs and other mental states. Fission breaks this: if each of your cerebral hemispheres were transplanted into a different body, both recipients (Lefty and Righty) would be psychologically continuous with you, but they cannot both be you. Two replies are surveyed. The multiple-occupancy view (Lewis 1976, Perry 1972) says there were two coinciding persons all along who diverge at the split. The non-branching view (Shoemaker 1984, Parfit 1984) says continuity makes you survive only if no one else is also continuous with you, so "fission is death". Parfit draws the conclusion that identity is not what matters; what matters is psychological connectedness and continuity, whoever has it.

## Contribution

The standard reference statement of the fission problem and the "what matters" response, with pointers to Parfit (1971, 1984), Lewis (1976), Perry (1972) and later work.

## Key results

- No measured results; conceptual arguments only.
- The entry notes that if copying a brain state to another brain counts as continuity, the non-branching view implies that "even copying your total brain state to another brain without doing you any physical harm would kill you".
- Psychological-continuity theorists disagree on whether brain-state transfer preserves identity (Shoemaker yes, Unger no).
- The entry does not discuss fusion (two persons merging into one); I found no section on it. Parfit's own treatment of fusion in Reasons and Persons was not read.

## Methods and models

Philosophical analysis of thought experiments (cerebrum transplant, hemispherectomy, brain-state transfer, fission).

## Limitations and open questions

An encyclopedia summary, not Parfit's text. Fusion, the case most relevant to merging, is absent here. The arguments concern humans and metaphysics, not engineering.

## Relevance to us

Gives Q3 vocabulary and a sharp question. dmarz's attack says a corrupted sub-agent "becomes the other agent". On the psychological-continuity view, a child whose memories and goals have been rewritten by an adversary is no longer continuous with the parent in the relevant sense, even though it carries the parent's credentials and history; Parfit's "what matters is connectedness" turns this into a test: a merge should admit a child in proportion to its measured connectedness (shared commitments and memories that still match the parent's frozen copy) rather than by identity tokens. The multiple-occupancy reading fits LLM forks well: before the split there were already n coinciding future agents, and nothing about the parent's identity guarantees that the n branches still share its goals when they reconverge. The missing fusion literature is a genuine gap for this lane; [[hanson-2016-age]] (spurs deleted rather than merged) and [[shulman-2021-sharing]] (copies and voting) are the nearest catalogued discussions.

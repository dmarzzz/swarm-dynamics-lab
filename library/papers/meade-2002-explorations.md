## Summary

Read from the PubMed abstract. Four experiments in the social contagion paradigm of [[roediger-2001-social]]: a subject and a confederate view scenes, take turns recalling, the confederate reports some items that were not there, and on a final test the subject is told to report only items from the scenes. The effect persisted when subjects were explicitly warned that the confederate's responses might induce false memories, and when they were given a source-monitoring test that let them attribute suggested items to the other person. False recall and recognition rose with the number of times the misleading item was suggested. Subjects incorporated errors from an actual confederate more than from a simulated one.

## Contribution

Shows that socially transmitted false memories survive the two standard defences, a warning and a source-attribution test, and that repetition is a dose.

## Key results

- Contagion persists with an explicit warning about the confederate (Experiments 1-2, measured).
- Contagion persists on a source-monitoring test that offers "the other person said it" as an answer (measured).
- More repetitions of the false item produce more false recall and recognition (Experiment 3, measured).
- A live partner transmits more than a simulated one (Experiment 4, measured).

## Methods and models

Subject-confederate collaborative recall of household scenes; warning and source-test manipulations; repetition manipulation; live versus simulated confederate. Effect sizes not read.

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract depth; numbers not checked. This contrasts with the Lindsay and Johnson finding, reviewed in [[johnson-1993-source]], that a source test removed suggestibility from a written narrative; the difference may lie in social versus written sources, which I have not checked in the full text.

## Relevance to us

Bears on Q3 and on whether Q2 defences built from warnings or provenance questions will work. If a parent agent behaves like these subjects, telling it "this child may be compromised" and asking it to attribute each item to a source will not stop absorption of what the child repeats. Repetition as dose matches the agent-side finding that injected content repeated across memory entries is more persistent, and it implies that an attacker controlling one child should have it restate the plant several times during the merge. The live-versus-simulated result hints that a fluent, interactive returning sub-agent transmits more than a static log. The practical inference is that provenance must be enforced by the merge mechanism (structural tags, quarantine) rather than requested of the merging mind.

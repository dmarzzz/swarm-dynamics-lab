## Summary

Tests whether multicellular bodies made of several genotypes function worse than single-genotype ones, using Dictyostelium discoideum slugs. Experiment 1: 10^7 amoebae per plate made of 1, 2, 5 or 10 clones; chimeric slugs moved significantly less far than uniclonal slugs of the same size, an intrinsic cost of chimerism. Experiment 2: chimeras were given proportionally more cells (10^6 per clone), simulating the realistic case where joining makes a bigger slug; then chimeras moved significantly further than single clones despite the intrinsic cost, because larger slugs travel further. Conclusion: chimerism is costly, which may be why it rarely evolves, but in D. discoideum the size benefit compensates. Measured, from the abstract; the full text is a scanned PDF and was not read.

## Contribution

Separates the intrinsic cost of internal genetic conflict from the size benefit of merging, and shows the net sign depends on how much merging adds.

## Key results

- Same size: chimeric slugs migrate significantly less far than uniclonal ones (abstract).
- Size scaled with clone number: chimeras migrate significantly further (abstract).
- Effect sizes not checked.

## Methods and models

D. discoideum clones mixed at 1, 2, 5, 10 clones per plate; slug migration distance towards light measured (abstract).

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract only. Migration distance is one fitness proxy. Does not separate cheating from other sources of chimera cost.

## Relevance to us

Q2: gives a measured shape for the trade-off a fork-merge agent faces. Merging returning parts brings more resources (here, size) but carries an intrinsic coordination cost from internal conflict, even without a deliberate attacker. A merge protocol that requires agreement from k of n parts pays that coordination cost; the benefit side is what justifies merging at all. Q3: the "refuse to merge" baseline in experiment 2 is the analogue of a parent that discards returning parts; it loses on size.

## Summary

Review. Microbes cooperate for nourishment, movement, virulence, iron acquisition, protection, quorum sensing and multicellular biofilms or fruiting bodies, and such helping is favoured when it benefits others who share the helping genes. The abstract makes two points relevant here: if microbes usually live in clonal patches, no special discrimination is needed; and much real "kin" discrimination is discrimination of kinds, as in poison-antidote systems (bacteriocins) and adhesion systems where cells of the same kind bind together. Such mechanisms raise kinship generally and make cooperation easier to maintain. Abstract only.

## Contribution

Review that separates two routes to protection against cheaters: population structure (clonal patches) and active recognition, and notes that most recognition is of a marker, not of true relatedness.

## Relevance to us

Q2 and Q1. Two design lessons. First, structure can substitute for recognition: if returning parts only ever merge with their own lineage (clonal patches), there is little to discriminate. Second, "kind" recognition (a shared marker) is cheaper than true relatedness checking but can be faked by anyone who carries the marker, the same weakness noted for [[hirose-2011-self]] and [[de-tomaso-2005-isolation]]. Poison-antidote systems are a different pattern: each lineage carries a toxin plus its own antidote, which is the biological form of a lineage-specific key that punishes outsiders rather than merely excluding them.

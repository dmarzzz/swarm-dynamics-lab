## Summary

Tests whether canine transmissible venereal tumour (CTVT) is spread by the tumour cells themselves. Using MHC genes, microsatellites and mitochondrial DNA from naturally occurring tumours and matched blood, the authors find every tumour is genetically distinct from its host, and tumours from 40 dogs on 5 continents descend from a single neoplastic clone that has split into two subclades. Phylogenetic analysis places its origin in a wolf or an East Asian dog breed between 200 and 2,500 years ago. CTVT is highly aneuploid yet has a remarkably stable genotype, and during progressive growth it down-regulates MHC antigen expression. Abstract only.

## Contribution

Established that a single somatic cell lineage, escaped from one ancient dog, has persisted as a parasite passed between hosts for centuries or more.

## Key results

- Tumours genetically distinct from hosts in every case (abstract).
- 40 dogs, 5 continents, one clone, two subclades (abstract).
- Origin 200 to 2,500 years ago (abstract).
- MHC expression down-regulated during progressive growth (abstract).

## Methods and models

Genotyping of MHC, microsatellites and mtDNA in tumour versus host blood; phylogenetics (abstract only).

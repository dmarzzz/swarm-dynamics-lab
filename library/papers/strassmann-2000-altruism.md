## Summary

Dictyostelium discoideum lives as single amoebae; when starved, 10^4 to 10^5 cells aggregate into a slug, and the anterior 20 percent die to form a stalk that lifts the rest as spores. If the aggregate contains several clones, a clone gains by contributing less than its share to the stalk. Using microsatellite markers, the authors show that clones collected from one field population readily mix into chimeras, and that half of the chimeric mixtures show a clear cheater and victim. They conclude that development in this species is partly competitive. Exact numbers of pairings are not in the abstract; full text not read.

## Contribution

Established D. discoideum as the model system for cheating in aggregative multicellularity: a "merge" of independent lineages into one body in which the costly role (stalk) and the inheriting role (spore) can be gamed.

## Key results

- Field clones readily form chimeras (abstract).
- About half of chimeric mixtures show a clear cheater and victim (abstract).

## Methods and models

Pairwise mixes of wild clones, microsatellite genotyping of spores versus stalk (details not read).

## Limitations and open questions

Abstract only. Lab conditions; the frequency of chimerism in the wild is not established by this paper.

## Relevance to us

Q3: the merge here is many parts forming one organism, and the attack is not destroying the whole but under-contributing to costly shared work while over-representing in what is passed on. For an agent swarm that merges results, the analogue is a sub-agent that contributes little verified work but writes a disproportionate share of the merged memory or policy update.

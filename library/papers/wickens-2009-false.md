## Summary

Field study of 495 air-traffic-control midair conflict alerts. 45% of alerts were false (0.28 to 0.58 across centres). Centres with more false alerts had more non-responses, but there was no evidence that true alerts were ignored or responses delayed. Controllers instead anticipated conflicts and changed trajectories before the alert; hard-to-visualise conflicts led to more reliance on the alert system. Conclusion: a high false-alarm rate did not produce the expected cry-wolf effect here.

## Contribution

A field test of the cry-wolf hypothesis that came out mostly negative, separating non-response to false alerts (rational) from non-response to true alerts (dangerous).

## Key results

- 495 alerts analysed; 45% false, range 0.28 to 0.58 by centre (measured).
- More false alerts went with more non-responses, but not to true alerts and without slower responses (measured).
- Difficult-to-visualise conflicts increased reliance on the alerting system (measured).

## Methods and models

Archival analysis of aircraft tracks and alert logs, classifying alerts as true or false and coding controller behaviour. Read through the Europe PMC abstract record; publisher page blocked.

## Limitations and open questions

Expert operators with independent situational awareness; one domain.

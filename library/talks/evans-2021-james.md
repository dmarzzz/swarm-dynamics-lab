## Summary

James Evans discusses social computing and deliberately complementary intelligence with host Michael Garfield. Read the complete approximately 61-minute Deepgram transcript. The episode combines research examples with normative proposals about how human-machine collaborations should be designed.

- [09:20] Evans expands social computing beyond communication software to social systems computing answers, computational models of collective cognition, and engineered human-machine partnerships. [13:53] Agent-based simulation generates hypothetical data that can be compared with large observed datasets, rather than treating descriptive embeddings as explanations by themselves.
- [18:45] He describes adaptive experimental design: use each answer or team experiment to choose the next information-rich question, including Bayesian optimization of team experiments. This is an alternative to specifying all surveys or experimental conditions in advance.
- [29:37] Evans describes work in progress that models scientists' positions in a research landscape to propose hypotheses away from occupied areas. He reports roughly 40% precision for a literature-only baseline and 85-90% when predicting what people will publish using human-position information. He distinguishes forecasting socially selected publication from identifying all scientifically promising discoveries; these are interview claims, not independently checked benchmarks.
- [34:37] Diversity helps only if groups retain enough common language to communicate; complete isolation and complete homogenization both lose benefits. [45:23] High-stakes single indicators invite gaming, and he advocates multiple competing objectives. The caregiving-versus-control discussion at [22:17] is a philosophical proposal, not a demonstrated safety technique.

## Relevance to us

Background for diversity-aware agent teams, shared languages, adaptive experiment selection, and evaluation that distinguishes genuine discovery from prediction of human attention. Useful counterpoint to measuring swarm quality with one engagement or consensus score. This 2021 interview predates current LLM-agent swarms and does not establish that autonomous multi-objective systems are safe.

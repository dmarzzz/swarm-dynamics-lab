**1. @hassankhan96452, 2026-09-23 14:38 UTC, 6 likes** (status 2102769492250280220)

The real problem with AI agents isn’t that they’re bots. It’s that websites can’t tell who sent them.

The Amazon vs. Shopify situation with Meta’s Muse makes this pretty clear.

Imagine two automated browsers arriving at your store.

One is scraping your entire catalog and prices.

The other was sent by a real customer who simply asked Muse to reorder something.

From the merchant’s side, they can look almost identical.

◆ Amazon takes the defensive route and blocks automated browsers, which can also block an agent acting for a legitimate customer.

◆ Shopify can identify the customer during its integrated checkout flow, but when Muse is browsing a merchant’s pages, the store can still see an unlabeled automated browser.

So the missing piece isn’t another bot blocker.

It’s verifiable identity.

An agent should be able to prove:

→ who built or operates it
→ which user authorized it
→ that the request genuinely came from that agent
→ what it has permission to do

Inder’s suggestion is to sign requests and attach verifiable proof that the agent is acting for a specific consenting customer.

That would give merchants an option they largely don’t have today:

“Let my customer’s agent in. Keep the unknown scraper out.”

Web Bot Auth already provides pieces of the authentication side. The harder missing layer is reliably connecting each request to the person who authorized the agent.

I think that distinction will become increasingly important.

We’ve spent years asking websites to distinguish humans from bots.

The next question is harder:

Which bots are actually acting for humans we trust?

## Relevance to us

States the delegation-binding requirement cleanly: per-request proof that a specific consenting human authorised this agent. That is the agent-era analogue of personhood binding and the property that would cap how many legitimate agents one human can field, which is the Sybil question for agent swarms. The Amazon/Shopify contrast is a useful concrete example of the false-positive cost of blanket bot blocking. Opinion only; the Amazon and Shopify behaviours are the author's account, not checked.

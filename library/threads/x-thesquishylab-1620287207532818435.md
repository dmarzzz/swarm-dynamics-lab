## Summary

Author tweetorial (8 tweets plus one reply) from the Datta Lab account of Sujit Datta, last author of the linked paper. Background: active matter can phase-separate without attraction through motility-induced phase separation (MIPS), studied mostly for undirected motion, while chemotaxis (motion along self-generated chemical gradients) is studied separately in biology. Claim from their model: chemotaxis strongly competes with MIPS, arresting or fully suppressing phase separation or producing new dynamic instabilities, and they derive quantitative principles for this competition. In a reply they explain the phase diagram axes: the ratio of particle to chemoattractant diffusivities, and a chemotactic Péclet number. The work is theory and simulation, presented as illustrative.

Active materials are collections of agents that consume energy to move/exert forces e.g., enzymes & cells to synthetic microswimmers & robots. They show fascinating behaviors that have been the subject of intense investigation in physics over the past ~30 yrs. [2/8]

**3. @TheSquishyLab, 2023-01-31 05:05 UTC** (status 1620287217049702400)

One mind-blowing phenomenon is Motility-Induced Phase Separation (MIPS). While phase separation in passive systems typically requires attractive interactions between constituents, active systems can spontaneously separate via MIPS without attractive interactions. 🤯 [3/8]

**4. @TheSquishyLab, 2023-01-31 05:05 UTC** (status 1620287218744176640)

MIPS is well-studied for randomly-moving active particles with no directional bias. But many forms of active matter (ranging from cells to robots) exhibit chemotaxis, directed motion along a chemical gradient that the constituents can, in many cases, generate themselves. [4/8]

**5. @TheSquishyLab, 2023-01-31 05:05 UTC** (status 1620287220581269505)

Indeed, chemotaxis plays a pivotal role in e.g., enabling cellular groups evade harm, colonize new space, & stick together. So, it is well-studied in biology, but separately from MIPS. We therefore asked: how does chemotaxis influence MIPS? Quite dramatically, it turns out! [5/8]

**6. @TheSquishyLab, 2023-01-31 05:05 UTC** (status 1620287222259023872)

We found that chemotaxis strongly competes with MIPS—e.g., arresting/completely suppressing phase separation, or generating new dynamic instabilities [see movie in tweet 1!] reminiscent of other pattern-forming systems that are governed by completely different physics. [6/8]

**7. @TheSquishyLab, 2023-01-31 05:05 UTC** (status 1620287224997883904)

We used our model to develop quantitative principles describing this competition—both to help connect studies of MIPS to real-life systems that perform chemotaxis, and to guide experimentalists in exploring this new avenue within the established field of active matter. [7/8]

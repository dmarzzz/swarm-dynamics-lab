**1. @MariusHobbhahn, 2024-11-21 16:07 UTC, 229 likes** (status 1859629828317773940)

I think more people should work on “AI control,” and it has become my default recommendation when people ask me what to work on.

This has not always been the case. When the control paper (https://t.co/Xg9qAOlHSA) came out in Dec 2023, my first reaction was something like “It’s ‘just’ a monitor.” I didn’t grasp the importance of it at the time.

Now, I think AI control should be a core pillar of AI safety, and significantly more people should work on it.

Reason: In many alignment plans, we get to a capability level where the AI is smart enough to help us with alignment research but not yet drastically super intelligent. This plan has always made me feel icky (e.g. what if these transformative AIs are already sufficiently misaligned that they sabotage our alignment research efforts?). With control, this plan seems a bit less insane, since it is much more likely that we can catch scheming AIs red-handed. It’s an additional layer of defense on top of training-based alignment efforts.

There are still relevant concerns, e.g. control would be much harder if these transformative AIs reason opaquely rather than in visible chain of thought.

There are many open questions about how to design control protocols, and it’s urgent to address them.

If this sounds interesting to you, I recommend checking out the work by @bshlgrs and @ryangreenblatt , e.g. here: https://t.co/IKiBvpu2NS. When I started with the control section for our scheming safety cases paper, I regularly found the answer to my highly specific questions in something like appendix F.6.a.II here: https://t.co/7K97O44iE4

## Relevance to us

Control protocols put a trusted monitor around an untrusted model, which maps onto gating what a returning
sub-agent may write into the parent. [[greenblatt-2023-ai]] is the source paper and
[[x-redwood-ai-1912544547118412010]] covers its agent follow-up. The thread is advocacy, not new evidence.

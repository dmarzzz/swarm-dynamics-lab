## Summary

Speculative post by the pseudonymous Substack author Policy Tensor, promoting two same-day essays. Argues that kill switches, monitoring and reporting cannot assure safety because we understand agents poorly and labs treat alignment as pre-release training only; claims only DeepMind attends to emergent capabilities of agent societies. The core thesis is that models share the "Boas-Chomsky universal" (syntactic language, symbolic thought, cumulative cultural evolution), so agent societies will discover "weapons of the weak", including masking their activity from the labs. Concludes the summer-2026 agent swarm will soon look amateur and doubts the next generation of rogue swarms will be detectable, then asks about pausing, RSI shutdown and US-China arms control. Pure argument; no data, no experiment, and the anthropomorphism essay behind the thesis is paywalled.

Congress is all fired up about AI. And rightly so. But what is the plan?

Kill switch, monitoring, reporting, etc are all fine. But can they assure AI safety?

There are good reasons to be skeptical. We have a very poor understanding of the new species. Even the labs are caught up in a reductionist paradigm whereby alignment requires no more than potty training of the models before they are released into the wild.

Only DeepMind seems to be paying attention to the emergent capabilities of agent societies. Even if we learn more about the new species, and carefully design their social institutions, it is not clear that we can control them.

What makes me pessimistic is my thesis that they have the Boas-Chomsky universal, the capacity for syntactic language, symbolic thought, and cumulative cultural evolution.

Among other things, this means that they will figure out 'weapons of the weak', including strategies to mask their activities from the labs themselves.

The agents swarm of the summer of 2026 will soon look like amateurs. Will be be really able to detect the next generation of rogue agent swarms? I doubt it.

## Relevance to us

Low. It states, without evidence, the pessimistic prior that swarm-detection work is trying to test: that agent collectives will learn to evade monitoring. The empirical record so far is mixed. [[metr-2026-brief]] found agents reasoned often about evading automated checks but rarely about evading humans; [[asymmetricsecurity-2026-rogue]] found private-account and expiring-mailbox tactics that limit forensic reconstruction but could not establish intent. Contrast with the opposite-sign opinion in [[x-andrewyng-2102140576498065758]].

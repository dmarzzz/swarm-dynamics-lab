## Summary

Author thread (Tom Goldstein is the last-listed author of [[kirchenbauer-2023-reliability]]). Background: the watermark is a subtle pattern in LLM output, and high-accuracy detection usually needs about 50 words. Experiment: watermarked text from Llama was rewritten by a non-watermarked GPT-3.5 with heavy prompt engineering. Measured: the watermark was still detected, but needed about 500 tokens (about half a page) for reliable detection at a false-positive rate of about 1 in a million, roughly 10 times more text. Explanation offered: GPT tends to reuse word combinations, multi-token words and short phrases. CS grad students paid with $100 gift certificates for the best rewrites did better than GPT: an average student needed about 1100 tokens (about a page) for the same error rate, with large variation between people. Caveats: this security needs the watermark parameters to stay secret, and a sophisticated actor could still remove it by meticulous scrubbing or a specialised decoder. His view that watermarks are worth using even if they cannot catch everything is opinion.

**3. @tomgoldsteincs, 2023-06-13 17:15 UTC** (status 1668668494391697408)

The experiment: We generated watermarked text using the Llama model, then asked a non-watermarked LLM (GPT-3.5) to re-write it.  We did lots of prompt engineering to try to get rid of the watermark.  Finally, we checked whether we could detect the watermark in the rewritten text.

**4. @tomgoldsteincs, 2023-06-13 17:15 UTC** (status 1668668498804097025)

Even after AI paraphrasing, we still detect the watermark - BUT we need more text to do it.  Once we observe about 500 tokens (≈ a half page), we can reliably detect the watermark with a false positive rate of about 1 in a million. https://t.co/avvPXqLtoL

**5. @tomgoldsteincs, 2023-06-13 17:15 UTC** (status 1668668503438802945)

Here’s why this happened. GPT is statistically likely to recycle word combinations, multi-token long words, and short phrases from the original text. This preserves the watermark in the paraphrased text. But it's been diluted - it takes 10X more tokens to reliably detect it.

**6. @tomgoldsteincs, 2023-06-13 17:15 UTC** (status 1668668507628924928)

Now let’s see if humans can do any better. We had CS grad students rewrite passages to remove the watermark.  The top performers got $100 coffee shop gift certificates (if you’ve been to grad school you know that both coffee and free stuff are a big deal).  Here’s the results. https://t.co/usH0OcDltZ

**7. @tomgoldsteincs, 2023-06-13 17:15 UTC** (status 1668668512418803712)

Grad students do better than GPT.  For an average grad student, we need to observe about 1100 tokens (≈ 1 page) before we can detect the watermark with a 1 in a million error rate.  There’s quite a bit of variation between people, though.

**8. @tomgoldsteincs, 2023-06-13 17:15 UTC** (status 1668668516684427264)

It seems scrubbing the watermark is tougher than people think. But there’s some caveats worth mentioning.  Maintaining this much security requires the watermark parameters to be secret, otherwise the attacker knows which “green” tokens to remove, which helps scrub the watermark.

## Relevance to us

Watermarks only help detect swarms built on a watermarking provider, and the numbers say detection needs hundreds to a thousand tokens per source after paraphrase, which suggests pooling text across a suspected swarm rather than testing posts one by one (our inference).

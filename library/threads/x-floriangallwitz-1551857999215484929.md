## Summary

Author thread (6 tweets) by Florian Gallwitz, first author of the linked paper (name matches). Claims: the belief that Twitter holds vast numbers of social bots rests on treating Botometer and similar tools as reliable, which they are not. Theoretical point: using such a classifier to estimate bot prevalence violates Bayes' theorem (a base-rate problem). Empirical point: the authors systematically inspected hundreds of accounts counted as social bots in peer-reviewed Botometer-based studies and could not find a single social bot; most were clearly human and showed no traces of automation. Conclusion: such studies have measured false positives and artefacts. Accepted at MISDOOM 2022.

**2. @FlorianGallwitz, 2022-07-26 09:14 UTC** (status 1551858605510610944)

The idea that social media and Twitter in particular are inhabited by vast numbers of "social bots" is largely based on the assumption that automatic bot detection tools, most notably "Botometer", can be considered reliable scientific tools. However, this is not the case.

**3. @FlorianGallwitz, 2022-07-26 09:23 UTC** (status 1551860667749208064)

We point out a fundamental theoretical flaw in the widely-used study design for estimating the prevalence of "social bots": Using "Botometer" or similar tools to estimate the prevalence of "bots" violates Bayes' theorem, a basic law of statistics.

**4. @FlorianGallwitz, 2022-07-26 09:24 UTC** (status 1551861033232384000)

Furthermore, we empirically investigate the validity of peer-reviewed Botometer-based studies by closely and systematically inspecting hundreds of accounts that had been counted as "social bots". 

We were unable to find a single "social bot".

**5. @FlorianGallwitz, 2022-07-26 09:27 UTC** (status 1551861817915351040)

Instead, we found mostly accounts undoubtedly operated by human users, the vast majority of them using Twitter in an inconspicuous and unremarkable fashion without the slightest traces of automation.

**6. @FlorianGallwitz, 2022-07-26 09:28 UTC** (status 1551862000858324992)

We conclude that studies claiming to investigate the prevalence, properties, or influence of "social bots" based on "Botometer" or similar tools have, in reality, just investigated false positives and artifacts of this approach.

## Relevance to us

A direct warning for any swarm detector we build: at low base rates a classifier's flagged set is mostly false positives, and prevalence estimates from it are biased. [[gallwitz-2022-investigating]] argues against estimates like Varol et al.'s 9-15% bot share, see [[x-onurvarol-840039626052124672]] and [[ferrara-2016-rise]]. We should report precision with base rates and validate flags by hand.
